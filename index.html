HISTORY IN GLOBALIZATION AND THE PLACE OF EAST ASIAN DESIGN

Wen Huei Chou

ABSTRACT

Engineering and Product Design Education Conference 7-8 September 2006, Salzburg University of Applied Sciences, Salzburg, Austria

To resolve East Asia’s marginalisation in design history, this research investigates the design history in Taiwan as an example, argues for design history’s reconception from deeper historical and globological perspectives, and also advocates the imperative adjustment of design history curriculum in the marginalized regions. The meta-discourses of Euro-American modernism and postmodernism constitute the roots of privileged and prejudicial knowledge in the history of design, establishing Western developments as the yardstick for the production of design history. Substantive frameworks for shaping design history are necessary; however, such frameworks are also subject to change as historical conditions alter. The recent rise of East Asian economies focuses attention on their culture, including their cultural heritage, which has had a world historical influence over fields as diverse as writing, printing and design. Acknowledging this historical influence is fundamental to the development of East Asian localities as distinctive design spheres, while opening up scope for culture-based knowledge and understanding to perhaps reorient aspects of homogenous Western design.

 

Taiwan’s sense of cultural marginalization, for example, not only involves the power and influence of Euro-American design but also its relationship to the emergent economic giant of China, which has led to Taiwan’s exclusion from the WTO and other international organizations.

Although I am happy to see the inclusion of Taiwan in discussions in design spaces, the tone and tenor used to describe the situation at hand is a little frustrating from a Hong Konger’s point of view.

This is an oversimplification of the problem and negates the agency of all other actors here. Even in the time period described in the article, Taiwan was not as helpless as they portrayed. Taiwan had strategic, military, and cultural significance; its impact on China (and the world by extension)  is not to be underestimated. 

Like all other nations, Taiwan’s design industry is a response to the global trend of industrialization and modernism. This is a prime example that no one can escape the “influence of Euro-American, and especially American culture as the hegemonic force”, and that included Taiwan. To make a statement that Taiwan (and by extension, other unrepresented Asian countries) is outside of the Euro-American grasp simply because it is undocumented seems like a silly sentiment.

The researchers in the article bring up that design “simply did not exist” before 1960s, but who was there to observe that fact? Who drew the benchmark that marked the start of Asian design? This idea alone seems condescending; as if Asian design only exists if it is recognised. Unless the authors would like to argue design (in general) did not exist before 1960s, this point of view seems questionable to me.
